## Plaster Walls in Older Bergen County Homes: What You Need to Know Before You Start Patching

If your home was built before 1960, there's a good chance you're living with plaster walls — and if you've started noticing cracks, bulges, or chunks of wall that seem ready to fall off, you're not alone. Plaster repair is one of the most common calls we get from homeowners across Bergen County, particularly in older neighborhoods in towns like [Ridgewood](/areas/ridgewood/), [Hackensack](/areas/hackensack/), and [Teaneck](/areas/teaneck/).

Knowing how to repair plaster walls properly can save you a lot of headache — and money. But it's also one of those home repair tasks where the gap between a small, manageable fix and a job that's way over your head can be surprisingly narrow. This guide will help you figure out which situation you're dealing with and what to do about it.

## What Causes Plaster Walls to Fail?

Plaster is remarkably durable — it's survived in Bergen County homes for decades, in some cases over a century. But it does have its vulnerabilities. Understanding why plaster fails helps you make smarter decisions about how to fix it.

### Age and Settling
Homes settle over time, and plaster doesn't flex the way modern drywall does. As a house shifts — even slightly — plaster can crack along stress points, especially around door frames, windows, and corners. In older Bergen County homes, this is usually a cosmetic issue, though widespread cracking can signal something more serious.

### Moisture and Water Damage
This is the big one. Plaster and water are not friends. A slow roof leak, a sweating pipe inside the wall, or even high humidity over many years can cause plaster to soften, stain, and eventually crumble. If you're dealing with plaster damage and there's any chance moisture is involved, you need to find and fix the source before you touch the wall.

### Failed Lath Keys
Traditional plaster was applied in coats over wooden lath strips. Over time, the "keys" — the small bits of plaster that squeezed between the lath strips and hardened to hold everything in place — can break loose. When this happens, sections of plaster lose their mechanical bond to the wall and start to bulge or separate. You'll sometimes hear this called "blown" plaster.

### Impact and Everyday Wear
Doorknobs, furniture, kids, pets — plaster takes hits over the years. Small holes and chips are common, especially in high-traffic areas like hallways and stairwells.

## Can You DIY Plaster Repair?

Honestly? It depends on the scope of what you're dealing with.

**Small repairs are often DIY-friendly.** A hairline crack, a small hole from a picture hook, or a minor chip at a corner — these are manageable for a homeowner who's willing to take their time and do a little research. The materials are available at most hardware stores, and with some patience, you can get decent results.

**Larger repairs are a different story.** If you're looking at a section of wall that's bulging, an area where the plaster has separated from the lath, or any repair larger than about six inches, you're getting into territory where professional skills and materials make a real difference. Plaster has a learning curve, and matching the texture and finish of old plaster is genuinely difficult — especially if you want the repair to blend invisibly.

The honest answer most contractors won't give you: try the small stuff yourself if you're handy. But know when to stop.

## Step-by-Step Guide for Small Plaster Repairs

For minor cracks and small holes, here's a straightforward approach that works for most homeowners:

**Step 1: Assess the damage.** Press gently on the wall around the damaged area. If it feels solid, you're probably dealing with a surface issue. If it flexes or sounds hollow, the plaster may have separated from the lath — that's a job for a professional.

**Step 2: Clean out the damaged area.** Use a putty knife or small chisel to remove any loose or crumbling plaster. You want a clean, stable edge to work with. Undercut the edges slightly so your patch has something to grip.

**Step 3: Dampen the area.** Old plaster is very porous and will suck moisture out of your patching compound too quickly if you don't wet it first. Use a spray bottle or damp sponge on the repair area.

**Step 4: Apply a bonding agent.** Products like Plaster Weld help new material adhere to old plaster. This step is worth skipping on drywall repairs but is genuinely important with plaster.

**Step 5: Apply your patching compound in coats.** For anything deeper than a surface scratch, don't try to fill it all at once. Apply a first coat, let it dry fully, then apply a second. Joint compound works for small surface repairs, but for deeper holes, consider a premixed plaster patching compound.

**Step 6: Sand and feather the edges.** Once fully dry, sand the patch smooth and feather the edges so there's no visible ridge. Wipe off dust before priming.

**Step 7: Prime and paint.** Always prime a plaster repair before painting. Skipping this step is how you end up with a patch that looks different from the surrounding wall even after two coats of paint.

## When to Call a Professional

There are some situations where calling in a pro isn't just the easier option — it's the right one.

- **Large or recurring cracks.** If a crack keeps coming back after you patch it, something structural may be going on that needs attention.
- **Bulging or hollow-sounding walls.** Failed lath keys mean the plaster has lost its support, and patching the surface won't fix that.
- **Water-damaged plaster.** Once moisture gets into plaster, the damage goes deeper than what's visible. A pro can assess whether the wall is salvageable or needs to come down.
- **Historic or decorative plaster.** Some older Bergen County homes have original moldings or decorative plasterwork that's genuinely irreplaceable. This work requires specialized skills and materials.
- **Any repair where matching the existing texture matters.** [Texture matching](/services/texture-matching/) is one of the hardest parts of plaster repair — getting it wrong means your patch stands out forever.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I use regular joint compound to patch plaster walls?**
You can use it for surface-level cracks and very shallow repairs, but it's not ideal for deeper work. Joint compound shrinks as it dries, which can cause cracking in thicker applications. For anything deeper than about ¼ inch, use a plaster-specific patching product, or build up in thin coats.

**How do I know if my walls are plaster or drywall?**
Knock on the wall — plaster sounds solid and dense, while drywall has a slightly hollow sound. You can also check around outlets: plaster walls are typically ⅞ inch to 1 inch thick, and you'll often see the layers (finish coat, brown coat, scratch coat) if you look at the edge of an outlet box.

**Is it worth repairing plaster, or should I just replace it with drywall?**
In most cases, repairing is worth it — plaster is actually superior to drywall in terms of soundproofing, durability, and fire resistance. Unless the plaster is extensively damaged or there's a moisture problem that keeps coming back, repair and preservation is usually the better call.

**How long does a professional plaster repair take?**
For most standard repairs, the work itself can be done in a few hours. However, plaster requires drying time between coats, so a complete repair including finishing and painting typically spans two to three days.
